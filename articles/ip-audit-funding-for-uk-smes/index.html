How much IP Audit funding is available?

The UK Intellectual Property Office (IPO, often called UKIPO) offers funding towards a professional IP audit through its IP Advance scheme. The current breakdown is:

IP audit fundingAmount (including VAT)
UKIPO contribution (subject to approval)£2,250
Business contribution£750
Combined funding and contribution£3,000

The funding covers the IP audit only. Any subsequent patent, design or trade mark work is separate.

Older guides may also mention IP Access. The IPO said in April 2025 that it would focus on IP Audit support for 2025/26; the current GOV.UK listing describes the audit only.

Who can apply for IP Audit funding?

The scheme is for trading businesses with fewer than 250 employees where IP is, or will be, essential to growth. Your business must also be engaged with a business support programme run by one of the IPO’s regional delivery partners.

You cannot apply directly to the IPO. Your regional business adviser submits the application, which is assessed. Meeting the criteria does not guarantee funding.

The regional routes are:

  • England: Innovate UK Business Growth
  • Wales: Welsh Government
  • Scotland: Scottish Enterprise, Highlands and Islands Enterprise, or South of Scotland Enterprise
  • Northern Ireland: Invest Northern Ireland.

See the official IP Advance funding guidance for the headline terms. The IPO’s April 2025 update published by CIPA explains the adviser-led process and regional routes. Check current availability with the relevant partner.

What is the position in England?

In England, the scheme is delivered through Innovate UK Business Growth. Your business must be receiving intensive growth support from an innovation and growth specialist. The audit forms part of that support.

As at 3 November 2026, new requests to Innovate UK Business Growth outside existing or committed client arrangements are paused. Support continues for existing and committed clients, including those receiving business growth support through relevant Innovate UK grant competitions. Check the current Innovate UK Business Growth FAQs before relying on this route.

If you already work with a specialist, ask whether an IP Audit would be appropriate and whether funding is available. If you do not, the pause may affect your access in England.

What does an IP audit actually look at?

A useful IP audit starts with understanding the business, not simply reading through a list of registered rights. What does the company make or do? Which technologies and processes matter most? Where is the business heading? The answers help determine which IP deserves attention.

An audit looks at what the business owns, what might need protection and where problems could arise. It can also identify rights that no longer justify their renewal costs. The aim is to find commercially useful answers, not simply recommend more filings.

Depending on the business, the review can include:

  • existing patents and patent applications, and new technical ideas that may be patentable
  • registered and unregistered designs, particularly where product appearance matters
  • trade marks, names and branding
  • copyright in drawings, software, photographs, website content and other material
  • trade secrets, confidential know-how and the way sensitive technical information is handled
  • ownership of IP created by founders, employees, consultants and external designers
  • assignments, licences, collaboration terms and other agreements affecting IP
  • disclosure risks, including discussions with suppliers, manufacturers, investors and customers
  • potential third-party IP risks that may justify separate investigation
  • a sensible order for further work, taking account of budget and commercial timing.

The funded audit includes a written report with recommendations, cost projections and a suggested timetable, followed by a meeting to discuss the findings. The report should help management decide what to protect now, what needs further investigation and what can wait.

Sometimes the most useful recommendation is a simple way for engineers to record new developments, so the business can assess potential inventions before they are disclosed.

An audit can highlight where a dedicated patentability, validity or freedom-to-operate investigation is needed. It does not replace that more detailed work.

What about mechanical engineering and physical products?

I am a mechanical engineer by training. Before establishing RIDAN IP, I spent more than three years in Dyson’s in-house IP team, following around 12 years in private practice.

For engineered products, the most valuable development may lie in the tooling, manufacturing process or test method, rather than the finished product. Patent protection may make sense, but keeping a method confidential could be preferable if disclosure would give competitors useful information.

A fast-moving product line may not justify the same long-term investment in patents as technology used across several products. During an audit, I look at what matters commercially, where the business is heading and what needs attention before disclosure. My in-house experience helps me weigh those decisions against engineering, manufacturing, launch and fundraising priorities.

When is an IP audit worth doing?

An audit can be particularly useful before investment, a product launch or entry into a new market. Bringing in new suppliers or development partners is another good time to review the position.

The problems can be quite ordinary. A founder may assume the company owns a contractor’s work without checking the contract. A product team may be close to public disclosure without having considered patent filing. A business may be paying to maintain rights that no longer support its products.

If investment is approaching, see also preparing your IP portfolio for investment. Ownership questions are covered in who owns the intellectual property?, and the choice between filing and confidentiality is discussed in patent or trade secret?.
