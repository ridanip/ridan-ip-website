Fractional in-house IP support. When does fractional support make sense?

Practical guidance for startups, SMEs and growing technology businesses considering experienced intellectual property support on a flexible basis.

Discuss your IP position ›View all articles

PUBLISHED 6 SEPTEMBER 2026

By Dr Ritchie Lewis Daniel, Chartered Patent Attorney and European Patent Attorney

Many growing businesses reach a stage where intellectual property decisions become more frequent, more commercially significant and more closely connected to product development, investment and business strategy.

The business may benefit from experienced IP support on a flexible basis. Fractional in-house support can provide continuity and commercial context while allowing the level and focus of support to develop with the organisation.

What is fractional in-house IP support?

Fractional in-house IP support involves an external intellectual property professional working with a business in a way that reflects an in-house role. The arrangement may involve regular support, defined projects or a combination of both.

The focus is wider than individual patent or trade mark filings. Support may include understanding the product roadmap, identifying intellectual property, coordinating advisers, reviewing ownership, supporting commercial discussions and helping management teams make informed decisions.

Why businesses consider it

As organisations grow, intellectual property decisions may begin to affect investment discussions, product roadmaps, collaborations, commercial agreements and future competitive position.

Some businesses want continuity and strategic input but do not yet have an established internal IP function. Others have an internal legal or commercial team but want additional specialist patent and technology experience. The appropriate model depends on the existing team and the work that needs to be done.

Supporting startups and scale-ups

Startups and scale-ups often need to make intellectual property decisions while products, teams and commercial plans are still developing. Support may involve invention capture, patent timing, disclosure planning, ownership checks and preparation for investment discussions.

A fractional arrangement can also help establish proportionate processes. The objective is not to create unnecessary administration, but to ensure that important decisions and information are less likely to be lost during rapid growth.

Supporting SMEs and innovation-led businesses

Established SMEs may have significant technical capability without a dedicated intellectual property team. Innovation may be distributed across engineering, research, product, manufacturing and commercial functions.

Regular IP support can help connect those activities, identify commercially relevant developments and review whether existing protection remains aligned with current products and markets.

Working alongside management teams

Management teams often need concise advice that supports a decision rather than a lengthy explanation of legal rules. Fractional support can help frame intellectual property questions in terms of business objectives, timing, cost, available options and likely next steps.

This may include contributing to portfolio reviews, investment preparation, collaboration strategy, product planning or decisions about where further specialist advice is appropriate.

Working alongside engineering and development teams

Valuable intellectual property often emerges through day-to-day technical work. Engineers and developers may not describe a development as an invention, and formal invention forms may not suit every organisation.

Regular contact can support a better understanding of the technology, recognition of potentially important developments and discussion of protection before external disclosure. The process should fit the culture and working practices of the team.

Coordinating external patent attorneys and suppliers

A business may already use external patent attorneys, trade mark attorneys, solicitors or overseas associates. Fractional support does not necessarily replace those advisers.

Instead, the role may include preparing instructions, coordinating technical input, reviewing advice in commercial context, monitoring budgets and helping the business decide what work should be commissioned. Clear division of responsibilities is important.

Supporting investment and due diligence

Investment and transaction processes may generate questions about ownership, pending applications, confidential know-how, third-party rights and the connection between intellectual property and the business plan.

Fractional support can help organise information, identify matters that may need attention and explain the portfolio in measured terms. Intellectual property remains one part of a wider commercial assessment.

Supporting collaborations and commercial agreements

Research collaborations, development agreements, supplier relationships and joint projects can involve background intellectual property, newly created intellectual property and confidential information.

Fractional support may help the business identify the relevant assets and commercial objectives before detailed contractual drafting begins. Specialist legal advice may still be needed for particular agreements.

Commercial flexibility

A fractional arrangement can be shaped around the business. Some organisations may want regular access for ongoing decisions, while others may need concentrated support around a product programme, investment round or portfolio review.

The arrangement should have clear objectives, responsibilities and communication routes. Flexibility works best when both the business and adviser understand what is included and when additional expertise may be required.

When fractional support may not be appropriate

Fractional support is not the right model for every organisation. A business with a substantial and continuous workload may benefit from developing a dedicated internal team. Another business may need only occasional external advice on a specific filing.

The right approach depends on the nature and frequency of the work, the existing internal capability, the technology and the commercial priorities.

What experience can add

Effective in-house style support requires more than technical knowledge of intellectual property law. The adviser needs to understand how decisions are made within a technology business and how intellectual property interacts with engineering, commercial and management priorities.

Practical considerations

Before establishing a fractional arrangement, a business may wish to consider:

  • The decisions or projects that currently need support.
  • The internal teams and external advisers already involved.
  • The preferred level and frequency of contact.
  • How confidential information and conflicts will be managed.
  • Which responsibilities remain with management or other advisers.
  • How priorities, budgets and outcomes will be reviewed.
  • Whether the arrangement may need to change as the business develops.

A practical starting point

  1. Identify the immediate need. Define the decisions, projects or portfolio issues that would benefit from support.
  2. Map the current resources. Consider internal capability and the contribution of existing external advisers.
  3. Set an initial scope. Agree priorities, communication routes and the expected level of involvement.
  4. Start proportionately. A focused initial project may help both sides understand the most useful working model.
  5. Review the arrangement. The scope can develop as products, teams and commercial objectives change.

Frequently asked questions

Is fractional IP support only for startups?

No. SMEs, scale-ups and established technology businesses may also find the model useful, depending on their requirements and existing capability.

Does fractional support replace external patent attorneys?

Not necessarily. Fractional support may complement existing advisers by coordinating work, providing internal context and helping the business make informed decisions.

Can fractional support help with investment discussions?

Potentially. Intellectual property questions often arise during fundraising and due diligence, and organised records and measured explanations can assist those discussions.

Does every business need in-house IP support?

No general rule applies. The answer depends on the technology, commercial objectives, frequency of intellectual property decisions and existing resources.

Can the support focus on a particular project?

Yes. An arrangement may focus on a product programme, portfolio review, investment process or other defined objective, subject to the agreed scope.

Can the level of support change over time?

Potentially. A flexible arrangement can be reviewed as the volume and nature of the business's requirements develop.

Conclusion

Fractional in-house IP support sits between occasional external advice and a dedicated internal intellectual property function. For some businesses, the model can provide continuity, commercial context and experienced support while retaining flexibility.

The strongest arrangement is likely to be one that reflects the stage of the business, works constructively with existing teams and advisers, and keeps intellectual property decisions connected to commercial objectives.
